Pappus of Alexandria & Book VII of the Collection¶

Historical Context: The Domain of Analysis¶

Working in Alexandria around 340 CE, Pappus of Alexandria stood at the twilight of Greek antiquity’s mathematical tradition. Book VII of his eight-book Synagoge (Collection) is devoted to the Topos Analomenos—the Treasury (or Domain) of Analysis, a curriculum of geometric problem-solving treatises authored by Euclid, Apollonius of Perga, Aristaeus, and Eratosthenes.

Among the lost works summarized in Book VII were the three books of Euclid’s Porisms (Πορίσματα), which investigated variable configurations whose invariant incidences remain fixed as points move along lines. To help readers reconstruct Euclid’s terse arguments, Pappus supplied 38 auxiliary lemmas (Propositions 119–156 of Book VII). Without employing modern coordinates, those lemmas quietly laid the entire foundation of nineteenth-century projective geometry:

  • Proposition 129 (Invariance of the Cross-Ratio under Central Perspectivity): If four concurrent rays issuing from an apex $O$ are cut by two transversal lines $a$ and $b$, the compound ratio of segments cut off on $a$ equals the compound ratio cut off on $b$.

  • Proposition 131 (Harmonic Division in the Complete Quadrilateral): Establishes the harmonic separation induced on a transversal by opposite sides and diagonals of a four-line figure.

  • Proposition 138 (The Parallel Hexagon Lemma): Establishes collinearity of the three cross-join intersections when the two carrier lines $a$ and $b$ are parallel ($s(a, b) = 0$).

  • Proposition 139 (The General Projective Hexagon Lemma): Establishes collinearity when the two carrier lines $a$ and $b$ intersect at a finite point $O$.

Why Straightedge-Only Incidence Matters¶

Throughout Euclid’s Elements, compass-and-straightedge constructions intertwine incidence (drawing a line through two points, intersecting two lines) with metric congruence (transferring lengths via circles, measuring angles). Pappus’s Hexagon Theorem is revolutionary because its hypotheses and conclusion involve point-line incidence alone:

  1. No circle is drawn.

  2. No distance or angle is measured—though Pappus’s own ancient proof ingeniously used compound ratios of segments (equivalent to Menelaus’s theorem or cross-ratios), the theorem itself is purely projective.

  3. In 1899, David Hilbert proved in Grundlagen der Geometrie that within any projective plane, Pappus’s Hexagon Theorem is logically equivalent to the commutativity of multiplication ($xy = yx$) in the underlying coordinate division ring (skew field)!

Proposition 138: The Parallel Carrier Lemma¶

Before proving the general oblique case, Pappus treats parallel carrier lines a and b—precisely the coordinate setting explored in GEOMETOR’s original 2023 pappus.py study:

\[A_0 = (0, 0), \quad A_1 = (1, 0), \quad A_2 = (3, 0), \qquad B_0 = (0, 1), \quad B_1 = \left(\tfrac{3}{2}, 1\right), \quad B_2 = \left(\tfrac{5}{2}, 1\right).\]

Here the Wildberger rational-trigonometric spread between the two carriers vanishes identically, $s(a, b) = 0$, meaning their intersection lies on the ideal line at infinity.

Let A0, A1, A2 lie on a ($y = 0$) and let B0, B1, B2 lie on the parallel line b ($y = 1$). Then the opposite cross-join meets M01, M20, and M12 lie on a single straight line p.
step Lay out the three collinear points A0, A1, A2 along y = 0 and the three collinear points B0, B1, B2 along the parallel carrier y = 1.
step Intersect opposite hexagon sides A0B1 and A1B0 to obtain M01 $= \left(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{2}{5}\right)$.
step Intersect A2B0 and A0B2 to obtain M20 $= \left(\frac{15}{11}, \frac{6}{11}\right)$, and intersect A1B2 and A2B1 to obtain M12 $= \left(2, \frac{2}{3}\right)$.
step Verify exact collinearity: all three points satisfy $-4x + 21y = 6$, lying on the Pappus line M01M12 with exact rational quadrances $Q(M_{01}, M_{20}) = \frac{1828}{3025}$ and $Q(M_{20}, M_{12}) = \frac{457}{1089}$.

From Parallelism to Projective Unity¶

In the real projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$, Proposition 138 and Proposition 139 are the exact same theorem: applying a projective transformation sending the ideal line at infinity to a finite line maps parallel carriers $a \parallel b$ directly to intersecting carriers $a \cap b = O$, and conversely sending any line through $O$ to infinity reduces Proposition 139 instantaneously to Proposition 138!

Even when one of the cross-join pairs itself happens to be parallel (for example $[A_0 B_0] \parallel [A_1 B_1]$ when $x(A_0)=x(B_0)=0$ and $x(A_1)=x(B_1)=1$), its intersection is an ideal point on the line at infinity—meaning the line joining the remaining two finite meets is parallel to that cross-join pair. construct_pappus_hexagon handles both finite and ideal intersections seamlessly in exact symbolic arithmetic.
