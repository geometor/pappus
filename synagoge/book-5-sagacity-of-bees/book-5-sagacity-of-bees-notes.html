Book V · Philological & Mathematical Notes (Zenodorus, Bees & 13 Solids)¶

Variational History, Sector Inequality & Archimedean Polyhedra

1. Pappus’s Honeycomb Preface in the History of Optimization (Hultsch I, pp. 304–308)¶

Pappus’s Preface to Megethius is the founding classical text of mathematical biology and geometric optimization. While Varro (Res Rusticae III.16), Pliny, and Aelian had admired the six-sided wax cells of bees, Pappus was the first to formulate the honeycomb as a constrained variational problem: maximize enclosed area $A$ for fixed wall perimeter $P$ subject to the topological constraint that congruent cells partition $\mathbb{R}^2$ without interstitial voids. Sixteen centuries later, Charles Darwin (Origin of Species, Ch. VII) hailed the honeycomb as “absolutely perfect in economising labour and wax,” and in 1999 Thomas C. Hales proved the full Honeycomb Conjecture—showing that even if arbitrary curved walls are permitted, the regular hexagonal grid still minimizes total perimeter among all equal-area partitions of the plane!

2. Zenodorus and the Sector-Tangent Lemma (Prop. 3, Hultsch I pp. 314–318)¶

Pappus’s planar proof in Props. 1–10 reworks and sharpens the lost second-century BCE treatise On Isoperimetric Figures (Περὶ ἰσοπεριμέτρων σχημάτων) by Zenodorus (also summarized more briefly by Theon of Alexandria in his commentary on Ptolemy’s Almagest I.10). Notice the deep structural kinship between Pappus V.3 and Ptolemy’s Inequality Lemma (Almagest I.10, modeled in geometor/ptolemy):

  • Ptolemy compares chords to arcs ($\frac{\operatorname{crd}\alpha}{\operatorname{crd}\beta} < \frac{\alpha}{\beta}$ for $\alpha > \beta$) using inscribed triangles and circular sectors;

  • Zenodorus and Pappus compare tangents to arcs ($\frac{\tan\alpha}{\tan\beta} > \frac{\alpha}{\beta}$ for $\frac{\pi}{2} > \alpha > \beta > 0$) by bounding circular sectors from inside and outside—without invoking any transcendental limit!

3. Sole Ancient Witness to the Thirteen Archimedean Solids (Book V, Prop. 19)¶

Before proving spatial isoperimetric theorems for the sphere and five Platonic polyhedra (Props. 20–43), Pappus preserves in Book V, Chapter 34 / Proposition 19 (Hultsch I, pp. 352–358) the only surviving Greek list of the thirteen semi-regular convex polyhedra (τρισκαίδεκα σχήματα ἰσόπλευρα μὲν ἀλλʼ οὐχ ὁμοιοπρόσωπα) discovered by Archimedes:

  • Four 8-faced / 14-faced / 26-faced truncations & cuboctahedra: truncated tetrahedron ($8$ faces: $4_3 + 4_6$), cuboctahedron ($14$ faces: $8_3 + 6_4$), truncated cube ($14$ faces: $8_3 + 6_8$), truncated octahedron ($14$ faces: $6_4 + 8_6$), rhombicuboctahedron ($26$ faces: $8_3 + 18_4$), and great rhombicuboctahedron ($26$ faces: $12_4 + 8_6 + 6_8$);

  • Icosahedral-dodecahedral family & snub solids: icosidodecahedron ($32$ faces: $20_3 + 12_5$), truncated dodecahedron ($32$ faces: $20_3 + 12_{10}$), truncated icosahedron ($32$ faces: $12_5 + 20_6$), snub cube ($38$ faces: $32_3 + 6_4$), rhombicosidodecahedron ($62$ faces: $20_3 + 30_4 + 12_5$), great rhombicosidodecahedron ($62$ faces: $30_4 + 20_6 + 12_{10}$), and snub dodecahedron ($92$ faces: $80_3 + 12_5$).

Johannes Kepler (Harmonices Mundi, Book II, 1619) reconstructed all thirteen semi-regular polyhedra directly from Pappus’s Book V face table via Commandino's 1588 Latin edition.
