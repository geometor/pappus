Preface to Hermodorus: Analysis, Synthesis, and the Thirty-Three Books¶

Synagoge Book VII, Chs. 1–3 (Hultsch II, pp. 634–636) · Original English Translation

1. That which is called the Treasury of Analysis, my son Hermodorus, is—to speak concisely—a special body of doctrine prepared after the composition of the common Elements for those who wish to acquire in geometric inquiries the power of discovering solutions to the problems proposed to them; and being useful for this purpose alone, it was written by three men—Euclid the writer of the Elements, Apollonius of Perga, and Aristaeus the Elder—and proceeds by way of analysis and synthesis.

Analysis, then, is the path from what is sought, as if admitted, through its successive consequences toward something admitted in synthesis. For in analysis, assuming that which is sought as having already come to be, we examine that from which this follows, and again the antecedent of that, until by thus retracing our steps we arrive at something already known or holding the rank of a first principle; and such a method we call analysis, as being a solution backward. And in synthesis, conversely, setting down as already done that last thing apprehended in the analysis, and arranging in their natural order what were there consequents as antecedents here, and fitting them one to another, we arrive at the final completion of the construction of what was sought; and this we call synthesis.

2. Now analysis is of two kinds: one seeking the truth, which is called theoretical, and the other procuring what was proposed, which is called problematic.

f. 0181 · p. 636

In the theoretical kind, then, assuming what is sought as existing and as true, and then proceeding through its successive consequences as true and as existing by hypothesis to something admitted, if that admitted thing be true, what was sought will also be true, and the proof is the reverse of the analysis; but if we encounter something admitted to be false, what was sought will also be false. In the problematic kind, assuming what was proposed as known, and then proceeding through its successive consequences as true to something admitted, if that admitted thing be possible and procurable—what mathematicians call given—what was proposed will also be possible, and again the proof is the reverse of the analysis; but if we encounter something admitted to be impossible, the problem will also be impossible. [Determination is the prior distinction of when, how, and in how many ways the problem will be possible.]

So much, then, concerning analysis and synthesis.

3. Now the order of the aforesaid books of the Treasury of Analysis is as follows: one book of Euclid's Data, two of Apollonius's Cutting Off of a Ratio, two of Cutting Off of an Area, two of Determinate Section, two of Tangencies, three of Euclid's Porisms, two of Apollonius’s Inclinations, two of his Plane Loci, eight of his Conics, five of Aristaeus's Solid Loci, two of Euclid's Surface Loci, two of Eratosthenes On Means. In all there are thirty-three books, whose contents up to Apollonius’s Conics I have set out for your review, along with the number of loci, determinations, and cases in each book, and also the lemmas sought, leaving no inquiry unexamined in the treatment of the books, as I supposed.
