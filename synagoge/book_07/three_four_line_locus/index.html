The Three-, Four-, and Multi-Line Locus Problem¶

Synagoge Book VII, Chs. 36–39 (Hultsch II, pp. 676–680; App. IV, p. 1258) · Original English Translation

In reviewing Apollonius's Conics, Pappus records the celebrated Three- and Four-Line Locus (ὁ ἐπὶ τρεῖς καὶ τέσσαρας γραμμὰς τόπος, pp. 676–680): if straight lines are drawn from a point at given angles to three (or four) lines given in position, and the rectangle contained by two of them bears a given ratio to the square on the third (or to the rectangle contained by the remaining two), the locus of the point is a conic section.

When generalized to five, six, or arbitrarily many $n$ lines using compound ratios (λόγοι συγκείμενοι)—so that no impossible dimension beyond the cube or solid parallelepiped need be invoked—the ancients left the higher locus unconstructed, bequeathing the exact challenge through which René Descartes launched analytic geometry in La Géométrie (1637).
