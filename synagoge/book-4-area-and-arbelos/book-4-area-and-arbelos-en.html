Book IV · Generalized Pythagorean Area Theorem & the Arbelos Circle Chain (Translation)¶

Synagoge Book IV, Prop. 1 & Props. 14–18 (Hultsch I, pp. 176–179, 208–232) · Original English Translation from Hultsch’s Greek

1. Proposition 1: Pappus’s Parallelogram Generalization of the Pythagorean Theorem¶

Hultsch Vol. I, pp. 176–179 (Book IV, Ch. 1 / Prop. 1)

Book IV opens directly (its preface having been lost in the archetype manuscript) with a striking affine generalization of Euclid's Elements I.47 and [VI.31]: neither a right angle in the base triangle nor similarity between the erected figures is required. Arbitrary parallelograms erected outward upon any two sides of any triangle sum in area to a third parallelogram erected on the remaining side, whose lateral edge is given in both magnitude and direction by the segment joining the outer intersection apex to the vertex of the triangle.

If ABC be any triangle, and upon its sides $AB, AC$ there be described any parallelograms whatever, ABDE and ACFG, and the outer sides DE and FG be produced to meet at H, and HA be joined, then the parallelograms ABDE and ACFG together are equal in area to the parallelogram BCML contained by base $BC$ and lateral edges BL, CM equal and parallel to HA. “And this theorem is far more universal than that proved in the Elements concerning squares on right-angled triangles.”
step Set out scalene triangle ABC with A $= (3, 4)$, B $= (0, 0)$, and C $= (7, 0)$, and erect arbitrary outward parallelograms ABDE (area $13$) and ACFG (area $15$).
step Produce the outer sides DE (parallel to $AB$) and FG (parallel to $AC$) until they intersect at apex H, and draw the transversal line HA across the vertex A to meet base $BC$ at J.
step Through B and C draw BL and CM equal and parallel to segment $HA$ ($L - B = M - C = A - H$), and join $LM$ with JN parallel to $HA$. Because $BL$ and $CM$ are both equal and parallel to $HA$, they are equal and parallel to one another ([I.30]), so BCML is a parallelogram ([I.33]) partitioned by JN into sub-parallelograms $BJNL$ and $JCMN$.
step Apply [I.35] (shear-invariance of parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels) twice on the left: parallelogram ABDE equals the intermediate parallelogram $AB\text{-}HA$ on base $AB$ between parallels $AB$ and DE, which in turn equals $BJNL$ on parallel base $HA = BL$ between parallels BL and HA.
step

By the identical double shear on the right, parallelogram ACFG equals $JCMN$ between parallels HA and CM. Adding the two parts gives

\[\operatorname{Area}(ABDE) + \operatorname{Area}(ACFG) = \operatorname{Area}(BJNL) + \operatorname{Area}(JCMN) = \operatorname{Area}(BCML) = 13 + 15 = 28.\]

Specialization to Elements I.47 (Right Triangle & Squares)¶

When $\angle BAC = 90^\circ$ in a $3$-$4$-$5$ right triangle and ABDE (area $9$) and ACFG (area $16$) are squares, the corner triangle formed at H is congruent to ABC rotated by $90^\circ$, making HA perpendicular to hypotenuse $BC$ with exact length $|HA| = |BC| = 5$. Thus BCML becomes the $5 \times 5$ hypotenuse square of area $25$ partitioned along altitude JN into Euclid’s windmill rectangles of areas $\frac{9}{5} \cdot 5 = 9$ and $\frac{16}{5} \cdot 5 = 16$:

2. Propositions 14–18: The Ancient Theorem on the Arbelos Circle Chain¶

Hultsch Vol. I, pp. 208–232 (Book IV, Chs. 19–26 / Props. 14–18)

“There is set forth in the ancient geometrical inquiries a proposition of the following kind, which appears marvelous to many” (φέρεταί τις ἐν τοῖς ἀρχαίοις πρότασις τοιαύτη, Hultsch I p. 208): Suppose three semicircles be described tangent to one another upon a single straight diameter—forming the curvilinear region bounded by the three arcs which the ancients call the arbelos (ἄρβηλος, “shoemaker’s knife”)—and into the space between the three circumferences there be inscribed an endless chain of circles tangent both to the bounding semicircles and successively to one another. Then the perpendicular dropped from the center of the first circle to the base diameter equals its own diameter, that from the center of the second circle is double its diameter, that from the third is triple its diameter, and so on without end according to the natural numbers exceeding one another by unity:

\[y_n = n\,d_n = 2n\,r_n \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots).\]

After stating that the proposition remains equally valid whether the two inner semicircles lie on the same side (the arbelos proper) or embrace one another externally, Pappus establishes three preliminary tangent-circle lemmas (Props. 15–17, Hultsch I pp. 210–224):

  • Lemma 1 (Prop. 15): If two circles touch at $\Theta$ and a third circle touches both at $E$ and $P$, the chord $EP$ produced meets the line of centers on the radical tangent at $Z$ where $ZE \cdot ZP = Z\Theta^2$.

  • Lemma 2 (Prop. 16): For any single circle inscribed tangent to two nested semicircles on baseline $AC$, the square of its center’s perpendicular height satisfies a fixed rectangle relation on the base segments cut off by rays from the outer semicircle’s endpoints ([III.36]).

  • Lemma 3 (Prop. 17): If two consecutive circles centered at $K_n$ and $K_{n+1}$ touch both nested semicircles and touch each other externally at $\Theta$, then the sum of the $n$-th altitude and its diameter stands to its diameter in the exact same ratio as the $(n+1)$-th altitude stands to the $(n+1)$-th diameter:

    \[\frac{y_n + d_n}{d_n} = \frac{y_{n+1}}{d_{n+1}} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{y_{n+1}}{d_{n+1}} = \frac{y_n}{d_n} + 1.\]
Let outer semicircle (O0, A) on diameter AC be divided at D into inner semicircles (O1, A) of radius $r_1$ and (O2, D) of radius $r_2$ ($R = r_1 + r_2$). Inscribe circles C1, C2, C3, C4, $\dots$ mutually tangent in the arbelos, and drop altitudes P1K1, P2K2, P3K3, P4K4 perpendicular to AC. Then for every $n \ge 1$, the altitude $y_n = |P_n K_n|$ equals $n$ times the diameter $d_n$ of circle $C_n$.
step Along baseline AC with $r_1 = 2$ and $r_2 = 1$ ($R = 3$), place A $= (0, 0)$, D $= (4, 0)$, and C $= (6, 0)$, and describe outer semicircle (O0, A) centered at O0 $= (3, 0)$ together with inner semicircles (O1, A) and (O2, D) centered at O1 $= (2, 0)$ and O2 $= (5, 0)$.
step Inscribe the base circle C1 tangent to all three semicircles (Prop. 18 base step, Hultsch I pp. 226–228). By Lemma 2, its center K1 $= \left(\frac{30}{7}, \frac{12}{7}\right)$ above foot P1 has radius $r_1^{(c)} = \frac{6}{7}$ and diameter $d_1 = \frac{12}{7}$, so altitude P1K1 satisfies $y_1 = \frac{12}{7} = 1 \cdot d_1$.
step Inscribe C2 tangent to (O0, A), (O1, A), and C1. By Lemma 3 (Prop. 17), $\frac{y_2}{d_2} = \frac{y_1 + d_1}{d_1} = 1 + 1 = 2$. Indeed K2 $= \left(3, \frac{12}{5}\right)$ has radius $r_2^{(c)} = \frac{3}{5}$, diameter $d_2 = \frac{6}{5}$, and altitude P2K2 $= \frac{12}{5} = 2\,d_2$.
step Repeat the inductive recurrence $\frac{y_{n+1}}{d_{n+1}} = \frac{y_n}{d_n} + 1$ (Hultsch I p. 228, καὶ τοῦτο συμβαῖνον ἐπὶ τὸ ἄπειρον ἀποδειχθήσεται): circle C3 at K3 $= \left(2, \frac{12}{5}\right)$ ($\Delta_3 = 15$) has diameter $d_3 = \frac{4}{5}$ and altitude P3K3 $= \frac{12}{5} = 3\,d_3$; and circle C4 at K4 $= \left(\frac{15}{11}, \frac{24}{11}\right)$ ($\Delta_4 = 22$) has diameter $d_4 = \frac{6}{11}$ and altitude P4K4 $= \frac{24}{11} = 4\,d_4$. Moreover, every center $K_n$ lies on the fixed locus ellipse with foci O1 and O0 since $|K_n O_0| + |K_n O_1| = (R - r_n) + (r_1 + r_n) = R + r_1 = 5$.

In Chapters 29–68 of Book IV (Hultsch I pp. 230–303), Pappus adds the odd-multiple corollary $y_n = (2n - 1)r_n$ when the second inner semicircle degenerates to a point at $A$, and then investigates the linear curves of antiquity—the spiral of :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` (proving the sector-area one-third theorem), the conchoid of :person:`Nicomedes <nicomedes>`, the quadratrix of :person:`Dinostratus <dinostratus>`, and three solid hyperbola/:term:`neusis <neusis>` constructions for trisecting any rectilinear angle.
