Book VIII · Philological & Mathematical Notes (Stevin, Rusty Compass & Hero’s Gears)¶

Inclined-Plane Statics, Fixed-Aperture Constructions & Alexandrian Engineering

1. Pappus’s Inclined Plane (Prop. 9) vs. Stevin and Galileo (Hultsch III, pp. 1054–1058)¶

Pappus’s Proposition 9 is famous in the history of physics as the only surviving Greek attempt to derive the law of the inclined plane geometrically. Modeling the heavy body as a sphere resting on a plane inclined at angle $\alpha$ to the horizon, Pappus balances the sphere’s weight $W$ hanging at its center $E$ against an applied horizontal/parallel force by treating the horizontal radius through $E$ as a lever pivoted at the contact vertical. Because this static model compounds horizontal traction $C$ on the level plane with an overhanging lever arm proportional to $1 - \sin\alpha$, Pappus obtains a ratio different from the frictionless modern sine law $F = W \sin\alpha$ later discovered by Jordanus de Nemore (De ratione ponderis, 13th c.), Simon Stevin (De Beghinselen der Weeghconst, 1586, via his celebrated endless wreath of spheres Clootcrans), and Galileo Galilei (Le Meccaniche, c. 1600). Nevertheless, it was directly by critiquing Pappus VIII.9 in Commandino's 1588 Latin edition that Guido Ubaldo del Monte and young Galileo sharpened early-modern statics!

2. Greek Witness to Hero’s Mechanics and Baroulkos (Hultsch III, pp. 1060–1073, 1104–1115)¶

Because Hero of Alexandria's three-book Mechanics (Μηχανικά) survived complete only in Qusṭā ibn Lūqā’s ninth-century Arabic translation (Kitāb fī rafʿ al-ashyāʾ al-thaqīla), Pappus Book VIII is our primary extant Greek witness to Hero’s Baroulkos, his classification of the five simple machines (wedge, lever, windlass, pulley, endless screw), and his geometric rules for laying out meshing toothed cogwheels. Equally precious is Hultsch III, p. 1060, which preserves in crisp Doric-tinged koine Archimedes's canonical epigram «δός μοι ποῦ στῶ καὶ κινῶ τὴν γῆν».

3. From Pappus’s Heni Diastemati to the Poncelet–Steiner Theorem (p. 1074 ff.)¶

Pappus’s section on constructions executed with a single compass opening (τὰ ἑνὶ διαστήματι γραφόμενα, Hultsch III pp. 1074–1078) launched a 1,500-year constructive tradition:

  • Abū al-Wafāʾ al-Būzjānī (10th c., Book on What Is Necessary from Geometric Construction for the Artisan) systematically solved regular polygon and parabolic constructions with a fixed-aperture compass;

  • Niccolò Tartaglia, Lodovico Ferrari, and Girolamo Cardano (1546–1548) contested their famous Milanese public mathematical duel over solving all of Euclid's Elements with a straightedge and a rusty compass;

  • Jean-Victor Poncelet (1822) and Jakob Steiner (1833) proved the ultimate projective culmination: once a single circle and its center are drawn on the plane, every Euclidean compass-and-straightedge construction can be completed by straightedge alone—bringing Book VIII’s instrumental geometry full circle back to Book VII’s pure straightedge harmonic and Pappus–Steiner constructions!
