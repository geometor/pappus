Book VIII · Architectonic Mechanics, the Baroulkos & Seven Inscribed Hexagons (Translation)¶

Synagoge Book VIII, Preface to Hermodorus & Props. 1–19, 23 (Hultsch III, pp. 1022–1102) · Original English Translation from Hultsch’s Greek

1. Preface to Hermodorus: Architectonic Mechanics & the Center of Gravity¶

Hultsch Vol. III, pp. 1022–1034 (Book VIII, Chs. 1–7 / Props. 1–4)

The science of mechanics (ἡ μηχανικὴ θεωρία), my son Hermodorus, being serviceable for many great matters in human life, has rightly been deemed worthy of the highest esteem by philosophers, and is zealously pursued by nearly all who devote themselves to mathematical learning. For it stands first in investigating the natural inquiry concerning the material composition of the elements in the cosmos, and examines the stability and local motion of heavy bodies: both why things return toward their proper places and how bodies may be impelled upward contrary to their natural tendency (παρὰ φύσιν) through theorems that draw their principles from matter itself.

The followers of Hero of Alexandria divide mechanics into two complementary parts (Ch. 1, Hultsch III pp. 1022–1024):

  1. The rational and theoretical part (τὸ λογικόν), woven together out of geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and natural philosophy;

  2. The manual and practical part (τὸ χειρουργικόν), exercised through metalwork, architecture, carpentry, painting, and every bodily craft belonging to these arts.

Whoever has been trained from youth in all the aforesaid sciences and has likewise practiced the manual crafts becomes the supreme builder and inventor of mechanical works (ἀρχιτέκτων). Yet because a single person can seldom master every science and every craft simultaneously, they advise one who wishes to undertake mechanical works effectively to rely directly upon the crafts in which he is skilled while grounding their principles in geometric demonstration. Most indispensable among all mechanical arts are those of the machine-makers (μηχανουργοί, lifting great weights to a height with small forces as the ancients called them), the makers of engines (ὀργανοποιοί, hurling stone and iron missiles across great intervals), the water-lifters (ὑδραγωγοί), the wonder-workers (θαυματοποιοί, imitating living motions by air, cords, or floating counterweights, as Hero describes in Pneumatics and Automata), and the sphere-makers (σφαιροποιοί, constructing water-driven models of the heavens after Archimedes).

Although certain purists asserted that Archimedes of Syracuse wrote nothing mechanical save his treatise On Sphere-Making—deeming geometry sullied if applied to corporeal crafts—Carpus of Antioch rightly replies (Ch. 3, Hultsch III p. 1026) that Archimedes composed forty mechanical inventions alongside his sublime demonstrations on equilibria and floating bodies, and that geometry suffers no dishonor whatsoever when she lends symmetrical form and unerring measure to useful arts.

At the foundation of all rational mechanics stands a single geometric concept (Ch. 5, Hultsch III p. 1030):

“We need not examine at present what ‘the heavy’ and ‘the light’ are in their ultimate essence, nor why bodies move upward or downward; rather, let us define the **center of gravity* (κέντρον βάρους) of each body to be a certain point situated within it, from which, if the weight be conceived as suspended, it remains at rest while borne and preserves its original orientation, neither tilting nor turning in any revolution.”*

From this definition—without invoking physical conjecture—Pappus deduces geometrically the centers of gravity of rods, parallelograms, and triangles (concurrency of medians at the centroid, Props. 1–4, Hultsch III pp. 1030–1048), including the beautiful invariance theorem (Prop. 2) that if points divide the three sides of $\triangle ABC$ in the same cyclic ratio, the inscribed triangle shares the exact same center of gravity as $\triangle ABC$.

2. The Inclined Plane, Archimedes’s Lever Saying & Hero’s Baroulkos¶

Hultsch Vol. III, pp. 1054–1073, 1104–1115 (Book VIII, Chs. 16–25 / Props. 9–12)

In Proposition 9 (Hultsch III, pp. 1054–1058), Pappus poses for the first time in extant ancient Greek literature the fundamental problem of the inclined plane (ἐπὶ τοῦ κεκλιμένου ἐπιπέδου):

Given a weight which can be drawn along a horizontal plane by a given power $C$, and given a plane inclined to the horizon at a known angle $\alpha$, to find the power required to sustain and draw the weight upon the inclined plane.

Modeling the burden as a uniform sphere resting at contact point $L$ on the inclined plane so that its center $E$ overhangs $L$ toward the downhill side, Pappus reduces equilibrium on the slope to an equivalent lever across the horizontal radius and vertical line of weight.

Immediately afterward (Propositions 10–11, Hultsch III pp. 1060–1073), Pappus treats the problem of moving a given great weight by a given small power—the mechanical demonstration that inspired Archimedes to utter his immortal boast (Hultsch III, p. 1060):

«δός μοι ποῦ στῶ καὶ κινῶ τὴν γῆν» — “Give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth.”

Following Hero's Baroulkos and Mechanics, Pappus designs an explicit compound gear-train (βαρουλκός) moving a burden of $1{,}000$ talents by an applied effort of $5$ talents (mechanical advantage $200 : 1$):

  • Parallel axles carry pairs of meshing toothed drums and pinions (τύμπανα ὀδοντωτά) with wheel-to-axle diameter ratios $5 : 1$, $5 : 1$, and $4 : 1$ ($5 \times 5 \times 4 = 100 : 1$, doubled to $200 : 1$), coupled at the input shaft to an endless worm screw (κοχλίας ἄπειρος) turned smoothly by hand without slipping backward;

  • In the closing chapters (Hultsch III, pp. 1104–1115), Pappus gives the exact geometric construction for engraving meshing toothed gearwheels of prescribed tooth counts $m : n$ by making their pitch circumferences proportional to the numbers of teeth, and reduces all five simple powers (πέντε δυνάμεις: wedge σφήν, lever μοχλός, wheel-and-axle ἄξων ἐν περιτροχίῳ, pulley τροχιλία, and screw κοχλίας) to the single principle of concentric circles and the balance.

3. Single-Compass Constructions & Prop. 23: Seven Hexagons in a Circle¶

Hultsch Vol. III, pp. 1074–1102 (Book VIII, Chs. 26–39 / Props. 13–19 & 23)

Turning to instrumental geometry (τὰ ἰδίως λεγόμενα ὀργανικά), Pappus observes that architects, stonecutters, and engineers constantly face practical constraints in the field: restoring the true diameter of a broken temple column from a surviving fragment (Prop. 13), constructing the conic through five given points on a cylindrical surface (Prop. 14), or executing plane constructions with a single fixed compass opening (τὰ ἑνὶ διαστήματι γραφόμενα, Hultsch III p. 1074 ff.)—inaugurating the fixed-“rusty”-compass tradition later carried forward by Abū al-Wafāʾ, Tartaglia, Ferrari, Cardano, and Poncelet–Steiner.

Chief among these instrumental constructions—where geometric analysis replaces blind trial-and-error (πεῖρα) with exact algebraic certainty—stands Proposition 23 (Hultsch III, pp. 1096–1102), which crowns both Book VIII’s architectural geometry and Book V’s honeycomb rosette:

Proposition 23 (Hultsch III, pp. 1096–1102): Into a given circle (H0, N) of center H0 and radius H0N $= R$, to inscribe seven congruent regular hexagons—one central hexagon V1..V6 concentric with the circle, and six surrounding hexagons C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6 erected outward upon the six sides of the central hexagon so that each surrounding hexagon’s opposite outer side has both endpoints fitted cleanly upon the circumference of circle (H0, N).
step Analysis (ἀνάλυσις, Hultsch III pp. 1096–1098): Suppose the seven regular hexagons of common side $s_6$ are already inscribed around center H0, with outer rim vertex N of top cell C1 lying on circle (H0, N). Because a regular hexagon decomposes into six equilateral triangles of side $s_6$ ([IV.15] cor.), stepping from center H0 to a vertex of central cell V1..V6 advances distance $s_6$, and stepping along the outer radial zigzag across C1 to rim vertex N forms an obtuse triangle with sides $s_6$ and $2s_6$ enclosing an angle of $120^\circ$!
step

Apply [II.12] (Euclid’s obtuse-triangle square law, where the projection beyond the $120^\circ$ vertex equals $\frac{1}{2} s_6$): the square on radius H0N exceeds the sum of the squares on $s_6$ and $2s_6$ by twice the rectangle $2s_6 \cdot \frac{1}{2}s_6$:

\[R^2 = |H_0 N|^2 = s_6^2 + (2s_6)^2 + 2(2s_6)\!\left(\frac{1}{2}s_6\right) = s_6^2 + 4s_6^2 + 2s_6^2 = 7\,s_6^2.\]

Equivalently, in Cartesian coordinates centered at H0 (as constructed in construct_honeycomb_isoperimetry()), rim vertex N has horizontal displacement $\frac{1}{2}s_6$ and vertical displacement $\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}s_6$, giving quadrance $Q(H_0, N) = \frac{1}{4}s_6^2 + \frac{27}{4}s_6^2 = \frac{28}{4}s_6^2 = 7\,s_6^2$. Therefore $R^2 : s_6^2 = 7 : 1$, so the side $s_6 = \frac{R}{\sqrt{7}}$ is given in magnitude from the given radius $R$!

step Synthesis (σύνθεσις, Hultsch III pp. 1098–1102): Given circle (H0, N) of radius $R$ (in our canonical $P = 12$ model, $R = 2\sqrt{7}$), construct $s_6$ such that $7\,s_6^2 = R^2$ ($s_6 = 2$). With center H0 and radius $s_6$, inscribe central regular hexagon V1..V6 ([IV.15]), and upon its six sides erect the six congruent regular hexagons C1–C6.
step By symmetry and [II.12], all twelve outer rim vertices (two on the outermost side of each of C1–C6, exemplified by N) have exact quadrance $7\,s_6^2 = R^2$ from H0, so every outer edge is inscribed cleanly upon circle (H0, N) without a single trial adjustment.
