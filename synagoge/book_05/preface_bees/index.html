Preface to Megethius: On the Sagacity of Bees¶

Synagoge Book V, Chs. 1–3 (Hultsch I, pp. 304–308) · Original English Translation

Collection of Pappus of Alexandria, Book V. It contains comparisons of plane figures having equal perimeter with one another and with the circle, and comparisons of solid figures having equal surface with one another and with the sphere.

1. God has granted to humankind, most excellent Megethius, the noblest and most perfect conception of wisdom and mathematical learning, and in some measure has also allotted a share of it to certain irrational animals. Now to human beings, as rational creatures, he has afforded the ability to do each thing with reason and demonstration; while to the rest of the animals, without reason, he has bestowed only that which is useful and serviceable to life according to a certain natural forethought. One may observe this in most other kinds of animals, and not least among the bees. For their good order and marvelous obedience toward the rulers of their commonwealth, as well as their diligence and cleanliness in gathering honey and their forethought and stewardship in guarding it, are yet more wonderful. For, believing as is fitting that they bring this portion of ambrosia from the gods to the cultivated among humankind, they have not thought it right to pour it out at random upon earth or wood or any other unshapely and disorderly material; rather, gathering the finest nectar from the sweetest flowers growing upon the earth, they construct out of these for the reception of the honey the vessels called honeycombs, all equal and similar to one another and contiguous, and hexagonal in figure.

2. That they contrive this according to a certain geometric forethought we may learn as follows. For they judged it wholly necessary that the figures lie contiguous to one another and share common sides, lest any foreign thing falling into the intervening crevices should spoil their work.

f. 0339 · p. 306

Now three rectilinear figures were able to accomplish this, I mean regular figures that are both equilateral and equiangular, for dissimilar figures did not please the bees. Thus equilateral triangles, squares, and hexagons can lie contiguous to one another and have their sides in common without dissimilar interstices; for these can fill up of themselves the space around the same point, whereas for any other regular figure this is impossible. For the space around the same point is filled up by six equilateral triangles and by six angles, each of which is two-thirds of a right angle ([I.32]), and by four squares and four right angles, and likewise by three hexagons and three hexagon angles, each of which is one and a third right angles. But three angles of a pentagon fall short of filling up the space around the same point, while four exceed it; for three angles of a pentagon are less than four right angles (each angle being one and a fifth right angles), whereas four angles are greater than four right angles. Nor can even three heptagons be placed around the same point contiguous along their sides; for three angles of a heptagon are greater than four right angles (each being one right angle and three-sevenths). Still more will the same reasoning apply to figures of yet more angles. Since, then, there are three figures able of themselves to fill up the space around the same point—namely the triangle, the square, and the hexagon—the bees through their wisdom chose the one with the most angles for their construction, conceiving that it would hold more honey than either of the other two. 3. Now the bees know only what is useful to themselves, namely that the hexagon is greater than the square and the triangle and can hold more honey when equal material is expended upon the construction of each;

f. 0341 · p. 308

but we, professing to possess a greater share of wisdom than the bees, will investigate something more far-reaching. For of all equilateral and equiangular plane figures having equal perimeter, that which has the more angles is always greater, and greatest among all is the circle, when it has the same perimeter as they. We shall first show that among regular polygons having unequal numbers of angles, yet equal perimeter, that with more angles is always greater.
