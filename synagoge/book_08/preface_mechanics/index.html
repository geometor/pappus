Preface to Hermodorus: Architectonic Mechanics¶

Synagoge Book VIII, Preface to Hermodorus, Chapters 1–4 (Hultsch III, pp. 1022–1028) · Critical English Edition

It contains varied and choice mechanical problems.

1. The science of mechanics, my son Hermodorus, being serviceable for many great matters in human life, has rightly been deemed worthy of the highest esteem by philosophers, and is zealously pursued by all who devote themselves to mathematical learning, since it is almost the first to touch upon the natural inquiry concerning the matter of the elements in the cosmos. For, being theoretical in general regarding the rest and motion of bodies and their locomotion, it explains the causes of things moving according to nature, while it constrains others contrary to nature, removing them outside their proper places into opposite motions by contriving through the theorems that fall under it from matter itself. The mechanicians of Hero's school say that one part of mechanics is rational and the other manual; and that the rational part is composed of geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and physical principles, while the manual part is composed of

f. 0033 · p. 1024

metalwork, architecture, carpentry, painting, and manual practice in these arts. Accordingly, the one who has been trained from boyhood in the aforesaid sciences, has acquired a habit in the aforesaid arts, and in addition possesses a versatile nature, they say will be the most excellent inventor of mechanical works and master-builder. Since it is impossible for the same person to master so many mathematical disciplines and at the same time learn the aforesaid arts, they exhort one who wishes to undertake mechanical works to employ the proper arts subordinate to each particular need.

2. Most indispensable of all for the needs of life are [mechanics preceding architecture] the art of machine-makers, who are themselves also called mechanicians according to the ancients (for by means of machines they raise great weights contrary to nature to a height, moving them with a smaller force), and the art of engine-makers necessary for war, who are also called mechanicians (for missiles of stone and iron and the like are hurled a great distance by the catapults they construct), and besides these, the art of those specially called machine-builders (for water is more easily raised from a great depth by the pumping engines which they construct). The ancients also call mechanicians the wonder-workers, of whom some work artfully by means of air, as Hero in his Pneumatics, others appear to imitate the motions of living beings by cords and sinews,

f. 0035 · p. 1026

as Hero in his Automata and Balances, others by means of bodies borne upon water, as Archimedes in his On Floating Bodies, or by water-clocks, as Hero in his Water-Clocks, which indeed evidently share in the theory of gnomonics. They also call mechanicians those who understand sphere-making, by whom an image of the heavens is constructed through uniform circular motion of water.

3. Some say that Archimedes of Syracuse knew the cause and principle of all these things; for he alone in our life applied a versatile nature and ingenuity to every art, as Geminus the mathematician also says in his work On the Arrangement of the Mathematical Sciences. Carpus of Antioch says somewhere that Archimedes of Syracuse composed only one mechanical book, that on sphere-making, not deeming it worthy to write on any of the others. Yet that marvelous man, celebrated among the many for mechanics and endowed with greatness of genius so as to remain exceedingly praised among all men, earnestly wrote upon even the slightest matters pertaining to the foundational theory of geometry and arithmetic; for he manifestly loved the aforesaid sciences so greatly as not to endure introducing anything extraneous into them. Yet Carpus himself and certain others reasonably employed geometry even for some arts; for geometry is in no way harmed, being by nature fitted to give body to many arts, by associating with them [being as it were the mother of the arts, she is not harmed by caring for engine-making and architecture; nor by associating with land-surveying, gnomonics, mechanics, and

f. 0037 · p. 1028

scene-painting is she harmed in any way], but on the contrary she manifestly advances these, while being duly honored and adorned by them.

4. Since mechanical science and art is of such a nature and divided into so many parts, I thought it well to write down more concisely and clearly, and to arrange in a better order than what has been written by our predecessors, both the theorems studied by geometrical reasoning [and the most necessary ones concerning the motion of weights] among the ancients and the theorems usefully discovered by ourselves: such as, given a weight drawn by a given [receiving] force in a plane parallel to the horizon, and given another plane inclined at a given angle to the underlying one, to find the force by which the weight will be drawn on the inclined plane (for this is useful to mechanical machine-makers; for by adding some further force of men to the force found, they confidently raise the weight); and, given two unequal straight lines, to find two mean proportionals in continued proportion (for by means of this theorem every given solid figure is both increased and diminished in the given ratio); and how it is possible, when a drum and the number of its staves [given or teeth] are given, to fit alongside it a drum having a given number of teeth and to find the diameter of the fitted drum (for this is useful for many things in the art of machine-builders because of the fitting of toothed drums). Each of these will become clear in its proper place along with other things useful to the architect and mechanician, if first we set forth in order the principles that hold together the treatise on centers of gravity.
