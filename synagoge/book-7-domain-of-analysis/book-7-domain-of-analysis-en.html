Book VII · The Treasury of Analysis, Centroid Rule & Hexagon Collinearity (Translation)¶

Synagoge Book VII, Preface to Hermodorus & Props. 129, 131, 138–139 (Hultsch II, pp. 634–688, 868–894) · Original English Translation from Hultsch’s Greek

1. Preface to Hermodorus: Analysis, Synthesis, and the Thirty-Three Books¶

Hultsch Vol. II, pp. 634–638 (Book VII, Chs. 1–3)

That body of doctrine which is called the Treasury of Analysis (ὁ καλούμενος ἀναλυόμενος τόπος), my son Hermodorus, is—taken as a whole—a specialized mathematical resource prepared, after the composition of the common Elements, for those who wish to acquire in geometric inquiries the power of discovering solutions to problems set before them; and it was written exclusively for this purpose by three principal men: Euclid the author of the Elements, Apollonius of Perga, and Aristaeus the Elder—proceeding entirely by way of analysis and synthesis.

Analysis (ἀνάλυσις), then, is the path from that which is sought, assumed as if already admitted, through its successive consequences toward something admitted in synthesis. For in analysis, assuming the thing sought as having already come to be (ὡς γεγονὸς ὑποθέμενοι), we examine that antecedent from which this follows, and again the antecedent of that, until by thus retracing our steps (ἀναποδίζοντες) we arrive at something already known or holding the rank of a first principle; and such a procedure we call analysis, as being a solution backward (ἀνάπαλιν λύσις).

In synthesis (σύνθεσις), conversely, reversing our course, we set down as already done that last point apprehended in the analysis, and arranging in their natural order as consequences what had previously been antecedents, and fitting them one to another, we arrive at the final completion of the construction of what was sought; and this we call synthesis.

Now there are two genera of analysis:

  1. The theoretical (θεωρηματικόν), which seeks after truth: assuming what is proposed as existing and true, we advance through its consequences, as though true and existing by hypothesis, up to something admitted. If that admitted endpoint be true, what was sought will also be true, and its proof will be the reverse synthesis; but if we encounter something admitted to be false, what was sought will also be false.

  2. The problematic (προβληματικόν), which seeks to supply something proposed: assuming what is proposed as known, we advance through its consequences, as though true, up to something admitted. If that endpoint is possible and procurable (δυνατόν τε καὶ ποριστόν)—what mathematicians call given (δεδομένον)—then what was proposed will also be possible, and again the proof will correspond inversely to the analysis; if we encounter something admitted to be impossible, the problem will likewise be impossible. That part of the inquiry which specifies beforehand when, how, and in how many ways the problem will be possible is called its diorismos.

The thirty-three books of the Treasury of Analysis up to Apollonius, arranged in curricular order (Hultsch II p. 636), are these:

  1. Euclid's Data (Δεδομένα, 1 book);

  2. Apollonius's Cutting Off of a Ratio (Λόγου ἀποτομή, 2 books);

  3. Apollonius’s Cutting Off of an Area (Χωρίου ἀποτομή, 2 books);

  4. Apollonius’s Determinate Section (Διωρισμένη τομή, 2 books);

  5. Apollonius’s Tangencies (Ἐπαφαί, 2 books);

  6. Euclid's Porisms (Πορίσματα, 3 books);

  7. Apollonius’s Inclinations (Νεύσεις, 2 books);

  8. Apollonius’s Plane Loci (Τόποι ἐπίπεδοι, 2 books);

  9. Apollonius's Conics (Κωνικά, 8 books);

  10. Aristaeus's Solid Loci (Τόποι στερεοί, 5 books);

  11. Euclid's Surface Loci (Τόποι πρὸς ἐπιφανείᾳ, 2 books);

  12. Eratosthenes's On Means (Περὶ μεσοτήτων, 2 books).

2. The Multi-Line Locus Problem & the Pappus–Guldinus Centroid Rule¶

Hultsch Vol. II, pp. 676–683 (Book VII, Chs. 36–42)

In reviewing Apollonius's Conics, Pappus records the celebrated Three- and Four-Line Locus (ὁ ἐπὶ τρεῖς καὶ τέσσαρας γραμμὰς τόπος, pp. 676–680): if straight lines are drawn from a point at given angles to three (or four) lines given in position, and the rectangle contained by two of them bears a given ratio to the square on the third (or to the rectangle contained by the remaining two), the locus of the point is a conic section. When generalized to five, six, or arbitrarily many $n$ lines using compound ratios (λόγοι συγκείμενοι)—so that no impossible dimension beyond the cube or solid parallelepiped need be invoked—the ancients left the higher locus unconstructed, bequeathing the exact challenge through which René Descartes launched analytic geometry in La Géométrie (1637).

Immediately thereafter (Hultsch II, p. 682), Pappus announces his universal Centroid Theorem for Solids and Surfaces of Revolution (rediscovered thirteen centuries later by Paul Guldin):

Figures generated by a complete revolution of a plane lamina about an axis not cutting it have to one another the **ratio compounded* of the ratio of the revolving areas and the ratio of the straight lines drawn perpendicularly from their centers of gravity to the axes of revolution—that is, the ratio of the circumferences traced by their centers of gravity:*

\[\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)\left(\frac{\bar{r}_1}{\bar{r}_2}\right) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad V = A \cdot (2\pi \bar{r}), \qquad S = L \cdot (2\pi \bar{r}).\]

3. Propositions 129 & 131: Cross-Ratio Invariance & Complete-Quadrilateral Harmonics¶

Hultsch Vol. II, pp. 870–877 (Lemmas III & V to Euclid’s Porisms)

Among the thirty-eight lemmas supplied by Pappus for Euclid's lost Porisms, Proposition 129 (Hultsch II, p. 870) proves that if three rays issuing from a common apex $O$ together with a fourth line through $O$ cut two distinct transversals $(A, B, C, D)$ and $(A', B', C', D')$, then the compound ratio of segments cut off on the first transversal equals the compound ratio cut off on the second:

\[\frac{AC \cdot BD}{AD \cdot BC} = \frac{A'C' \cdot B'D'}{A'D' \cdot B'C'} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \operatorname{CR}(A, B; C, D) = \operatorname{CR}(A', B'; C', D').\]

In Proposition 131 (Hultsch II, p. 874), Pappus applies this projective invariance to the complete quadrilateral, showing that opposite sides and diagonals cut any transversal in harmonic division ($\operatorname{CR} = -1$)—yielding pure straightedge harmonic conjugate construction (construct_harmonic_fourth()) and generating 81 collinear harmonic tetrads across the Pappus–Steiner web (S+, S-):

4. Propositions 138 & 139: Pappus’s Hexagon Collinearity Theorems¶

Hultsch Vol. II, pp. 884–889 (Lemmas XII & XIII to Euclid’s Porisms)

Crowning the Porisms lemmas are Proposition 138 (when the two carrier lines are parallel, $a \parallel b$) and Proposition 139 (when the two carrier lines intersect at an apex $O$). Pappus establishes collinearity by applying compound ratios (equivalent to Menelaus’s transversal theorem or Prop. 129’s cross-ratio invariance) without drawing a single circle.

Proposition 139 (Hultsch II, pp. 886–889): Let two straight lines a and b meet at O, carrying three points A0, A1, A2 on a and three points B0, B1, B2 on b. If opposite cross-joins A0B1 & A1B0 meet at M01, A2B0 & A0B2 meet at M20, and A1B2 & A2B1 meet at M12, then the three points M01, M20, and M12 lie on a single straight line p.
step Set carrier a with A0 $= (0, 0)$, A1 $= (2, 0)$, A2 $= (5, 0)$ and carrier b with B0 $= (0, 2)$, B1 $= (2, 3)$, B2 $= (6, 5)$, concurring at perspective apex O $= (-4, 0)$.
step

Form the three opposite cross-join intersections of hexagon A0B1A2B0A1B2:

\[M_{01} = \left(\frac{4}{5}, \frac{6}{5}\right), \qquad M_{20} = \left(\frac{60}{37}, \frac{50}{37}\right), \qquad M_{12} = \left(\frac{10}{3}, \frac{5}{3}\right).\]
step By Proposition 129, projecting from A0 and A2 onto cross-joins A1B0 and A1B2 equates their four-point cross-ratios; since the two ranges share their apex A1, the joins connecting corresponding points concur, forcing M01, M20, and M12 onto the single Pappus line p ($-7x + 38y = 40$).
