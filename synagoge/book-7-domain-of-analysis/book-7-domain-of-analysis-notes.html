Book VII · Philological & Mathematical Notes (Descartes, Pascal & Hilbert)¶

From Commandino’s 1588 Edition to Modern Algebraic & Projective Geometry

1. Five Modern Disciplines Ignited by One Book of Pappus¶

When Federico Commandino's Latin translation of Synagoge Book VII appeared at Pesaro in 1588, it triggered five distinct revolutions in modern mathematics:

  1. Analytic Geometry (1637) — Reading Pappus’s account of the $3$-, $4$-, and $n$-line locus (Hultsch II pp. 676–680), René Descartes (La Géométrie, 1637) translated Pappus’s compound-ratio condition directly into polynomial equations $P(x, y) = 0$, inaugurating coordinate algebraic geometry.

  2. Barycentric Integral Calculus (1635–1641) — Paul Guldin published the volume-of-revolution centroid theorem in his Centrobaryca; although Guldin initially doubted Commandino’s manuscript source, Codex Vaticanus Graecus 218 (Hultsch II p. 682) confirms the theorem’s late-antique authenticity.

  3. Synthetic Projective Geometry & Involution (1639–1640) — Girard Desargues extended Pappus’s cross-ratio and harmonic lemmas (VII.129–145) into involution theory, and sixteen-year-old Blaise Pascal (Essay pour les coniques, 1640) recognized that two lines $a \cup b$ form a degenerate conic, lifting VII.138–139 to any smooth conic section (Hexagrammum Mysticum, modeled in geometor.pappus.pascal).

  4. Conic Eccentricity & Focus–Directrix (VII.238) — Near the close of Book VII (Hultsch II pp. 1008–1013, Lemma to Euclid's Surface Loci), Pappus preserves the only complete ancient proof of the focus–directrix property ($PF / PD = e$ classifying ellipses $e < 1$, parabolas $e = 1$, and hyperbolas $e > 1$), which Kepler and Newton later placed at the foundation of orbital mechanics.

  5. Foundations of Geometry & Field Commutativity (1899) — In Grundlagen der Geometrie (geometor/hilbert, Chapters V–VI), David Hilbert proved that in any Desarguesian projective plane, Pappus’s Hexagon Theorem holds if and only if multiplication in the coordinate division ring is commutative ($xy = yx$). Thus Pappus’s humble Porism Lemma XIII secretly encodes the algebraic law of commutativity!
