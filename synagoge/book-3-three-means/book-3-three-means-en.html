Book III · Problem Hierarchy, Cube Duplication & the Three Classical Means (Translation)¶

Synagoge Book III, Chs. 1–27 & 28–43 (Props. 1–5 & 28–30, Hultsch I, pp. 30–84) · Original English Translation from Hultsch’s Greek

1. Address to Pandrosion: Theorem, Problem, and the Threefold Hierarchy of Loci¶

Hultsch Vol. I, pp. 30–32, 54–56 (Book III, Chs. 1 & 20–22)

Those of the ancients who wished to examine geometric inquiries with precision, most noble Pandrosion, judged it necessary to distinguish a problem (πρόβλημα) from a theorem (θεώρημα). He who proposes a theorem—whatever be the geometric matter set out and whatever attribute he claims follows from those hypotheses—invites us to behold how that consequence holds of necessity; whereas he who sets forth a problem bids us construct something not yet brought into view, and therefore stands under an obligation to determine by a diorismos when, how, and in how many ways the construction is possible. If anyone announces a problem whose construction cannot be carried out by the means he assumes, he errs doubly: first in mistaking the nature of the locus to which the inquiry belongs, and second in offering a defective demonstration to learners.

Now some of those who came to us purporting to have studied geometry with you set out an attempted plane construction for finding two mean proportionals in continued proportion between two given unequal straight lines—the classic Delian problem of duplicating the cube, since Hippocrates of Chios and Euclid (Elements XI.33) showed that if $a : x = x : y = y : b$, then $a : b = a^3 : x^3$. When we tested their construction rigorously (Chs. 2–19, Hultsch I pp. 32–54), their assumed intersection failed to meet the required proportional condition, because the problem is by nature solid rather than plane.

For we affirm (Chs. 20–21, Hultsch I pp. 54–56) that there are three genera of geometric problems (ἐπίπεδα, στερεά, γραμμικά):

  1. Plane problems (ἐπίπεδα) are those capable of being solved exclusively by means of the straight line and the circumference of the circle; they are justly called plane because the lines through which they are found have their generation in a plane.

  2. Solid problems (στερεά) are those whose solution requires the employment of one or more sections of the cone—parabola, hyperbola, or ellipse—for their construction demands surfaces of solid figures, namely conical surfaces.

  3. Linear problems (γραμμικά) comprise a third genus still remaining beyond these: for their construction requires lines other than those just spoken of, possessing a more varied and forced generation (ποικιλωτέραν καὶ βεβιασμένην τὴν γένεσιν) arising from irregular surfaces and interwoven motions—such as the spirals (ἕλικες) of Archimedes, the quadratrices (τετραγωνίζουσαι) of Dinostratus, the conchoids (κογχοειδεῖς) of Nicomedes, and the cissoids (κισσοειδεῖς).

Geometers consider it no small methodological fault (ἁμάρτημα οὐ μικρόν) whenever anyone solves a plane problem by conics or higher linear curves, or conversely attempts to force a solid problem—such as the duplication of the cube or the trisection of an arbitrary rectilinear angle—into straight lines and circles alone. Because conic sections are delicate to draw with instruments in the plane, the ancients also devised exact mechanical and neusis constructions for two mean proportionals (Chs. 22–27, Props. 1–5, Hultsch I pp. 56–68): the sliding three-plate mesolabe of Eratosthenes, the conchoidal verging ruler of Nicomedes, the rectangle-and-pivot rule of Hero of Alexandria, and our own semicircular construction equivalent in geometric effect to Hero’s.

2. The Three Classical Means & Their Unified Semicircle Construction¶

Hultsch Vol. I, pp. 68–84 (Book III, Chs. 28–43 / Props. 28–30)

After our friend the philosopher Hierius inquired further concerning proportional means, we set out the ancient doctrine of medieties (μεσότητες) expounded by the Pythagoreans, Eratosthenes, and Nicomachus. Three primary proportions stand first and most ancient among all ten:

  • A triple $(a, m, b)$ is in arithmetic proportion (ἀριθμητικὴ μεσότης) whenever the first exceeds the second by the very same difference by which the second exceeds the third, so that $(a - m) : (m - b) = a : a = 1 : 1$, whence $A = \frac{a + b}{2}$.

  • A triple $(a, m, b)$ is in geometric proportion (γεωμετρικὴ μεσότης, proportion strictly so called) whenever the first has to the second the same ratio that the second has to the third, $(a - m) : (m - b) = a : m = m : b$, whence $G = \sqrt{ab}$.

  • A triple $(a, m, b)$ is in harmonic proportion (ἁρμονικὴ μεσότης) whenever the excess of the first over the second has to the excess of the second over the third the ratio of the first extreme to the third extreme:

    \[\frac{a - H}{H - b} = \frac{a}{b} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad H = \frac{2ab}{a + b}.\]

How one may exhibit all three means together inside a single semicircle—establishing at one glance both the fundamental identity $A \cdot H = G^2$ and the strict ordering $A \ge G \ge H$ (with equality if and only if $a = b$)—is demonstrated as follows (Chs. 40–43, Hultsch I pp. 72, 80–84).

Let two unequal straight segments AD $= a$ and DC $= b$ be placed end-to-end along diameter AC of semicircle ABC with center O. Erect DB perpendicular to AC meeting the circumference at B, join radius OB, and drop DF perpendicular to OB at F. Then radius OB is the arithmetic mean $A$, ordinate DB is the geometric mean $G$, and segment BF cut off from the vertex B is the harmonic mean $H$ of $a$ and $b$; moreover, the tangent BT at B meets diameter AC produced at the external harmonic conjugate T satisfying $(A, C; D, T) = -1$.
step Lay out A $= (0, 0)$, division point D $= (9, 0)$, and C $= (25, 0)$ along diameter AC ($a = 9$, $b = 16$). Bisect AC at center O $= \left(\frac{25}{2}, 0\right)$ and describe semicircle ABC. Because $OA = OC = OB$, radius OB equals $\frac{a + b}{2} = \frac{25}{2}$, which is the arithmetic mean $A$.
step Erect the perpendicular at D to meet semicircle ABC at B $= (9, 12)$. By [III.31], $\angle ABC$ in triangle ABC is right, so by [VI.8] and [VI.13] the altitude DB is the mean proportional between $AD$ and $DC$: $DB^2 = AD \cdot DC = ab = 144$, giving the geometric mean $G = 12$.
step

Join radius OB and drop DF perpendicular to OB at foot F $= \left(\frac{7641}{625}, \frac{588}{625}\right)$. Since right triangles $\triangle ODB$ and $\triangle DFB$ share acute angle $\angle OBD$ at B, they are equiangular ([VI.8]), whence $OB : DB = DB : BF$. Therefore

\[A \cdot BF = OB \cdot BF = DB^2 = G^2 = ab \quad \Longrightarrow \quad BF = \frac{2ab}{a + b} = \frac{288}{25},\]

proving that BF is the exact harmonic mean $H$, and that $G$ is itself the geometric mean between $A$ and $H$ ($A \cdot H = G^2$).

step Observe the nested right triangles $\triangle ODB$ (hypotenuse OB, leg DB) and $\triangle DFB$ (hypotenuse DB, leg BF). Because the hypotenuse of a right triangle strictly exceeds either leg ([I.19]) whenever D $\neq$ O, we read off immediately $OB > DB > BF$, i.e., $A > G > H$ ($\frac{25}{2} > 12 > \frac{288}{25}$), collapsing to equality $A = G = H$ when $a = b$.
step Draw tangent BT perpendicular to radius OB, meeting diameter AC produced at T $= \left(-\frac{225}{7}, 0\right)$. Parallelism of DF and BT transfers the ratio on OB onto the diameter line, yielding the four-point harmonic range $(A, C; D, T) = -1$ that recurs in projective form in Book VII Prop. 131.

3. Golden-Section Specialization in $\mathbb{Q}(\phi)$¶

When the diameter segments stand in the square of the golden ratio, $a = 1$ and $b = \phi^2 = \phi + 1$ (where $\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$), the total diameter has length $1 + \phi^2 = \phi + 2 = \sqrt{5}\,\phi$, and Pappus’s semicircle exhibits algebraic closure over $\mathbb{Q}(\phi)$:

Here OB gives $A = \frac{\phi + 2}{2}$, the geometric mean DB equals the golden ratio itself $G = \sqrt{1 \cdot \phi^2} = \phi$, and the harmonic mean BF equals $H = \frac{2\phi^2}{\phi + 2} = \frac{2\phi}{\sqrt{5}}$, confirming $A \cdot H = \phi^2 = G^2$ and $(A, C; D, T) = -1$ at T with zero floating-point approximation in geometor.pappus.construct_three_means().
